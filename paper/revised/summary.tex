\section*{The setting, in one example}

A text classifier is a function from text to a label. Feed it a film review, it
answers \emph{positive} or \emph{negative}.

An \textbf{adversarial attack} changes the input so the \emph{model} changes its
answer while the \emph{correct} answer stays the same. A person reading both
versions would give the same label; the model does not. That gap is the
vulnerability.

TextFooler finds these by swapping words for synonyms, one at a time, choosing the
words the model leans on most. In principle:

\begin{ex}
The movie was \textbf{excellent}, I \textbf{loved} every minute. $\rightarrow$ positive\\
The movie was \textbf{superb}, I \textbf{adored} every minute. $\rightarrow$ negative
\end{ex}

\noindent In practice the output is rarely that clean. Here is a real one from my
data, on a news article:

\begin{ex}
\textbf{Original.} Fears for T N pension after talks. Unions representing workers
at Turner Newall say they are `disappointed' after talks with stricken parent firm
Federal Mogul.\\[3pt]
\textbf{TextFooler.} Fears for T percent pension after debate. \textbf{Syndicates
portrayal worker} at Turner Newall say they are `disappointed' after
\textbf{chatter} with \textbf{bereaved parenting corporations} Canada Mogul.
\end{ex}

\noindent ``Bereaved parenting corporations'' is not something a person would
write. The median GPT-2-XL perplexity of a TextFooler perturbation is \textbf{522},
where ordinary English sits in the tens. An input nobody would ever produce says
little about deployment risk.

Hence the idea this paper examines: hand the attack to a language model and ask it
to rewrite the whole sentence so it reads naturally, while staying adversarial.
This is now a common shape for automated red-teaming --- one model generating the
probes that test another. \textbf{My question is what such a pipeline actually
measures.}

\section*{Finding 0: rewriting is a trade, not an improvement}

Rewriting is applied only to attacks that \emph{already succeed}, so its cost is
directly measurable: how many survive?

\textbf{44.5\%}, across 32 model--attack--dataset configurations. More than half
the attacks are destroyed by being rewritten. What that buys is fluency: median
perplexity falls from 522 to \textbf{22.9}.

The mechanism is not mysterious. The words fooling the model are often exactly the
awkward ones. Repair the English and the model stops being fooled --- fluency and
adversarial effect are competing for the same words.

\section*{Finding 1: label validity --- the probe is not an attack}

I ran two rewriting prompts on identical data: a task-specific one and a generic
one. By flip rate the task-specific prompt wins two to one, \textbf{119/180 against
58/180} ($p = 1.7\times10^{-10}$).

Then I looked at \emph{how} it wins. AG News is news-topic classification, and the
task-specific prompt for it reads ``changing topic from world to sports''. It
instructs the model to change the label. A real result:

\begin{ex}
\textbf{Original} (business): Yukos warns it oil output is lagging. Beleaguered
Russian energy giant Yukos has warned that it will not produce as much oil as
expected this year\ldots\\[3pt]
\textbf{Rewrite:} \textbf{Lakers facing setbacks in NBA title defense, LeBron's
injury a major concern.} The Los Angeles Lakers are facing a difficult road ahead
in their quest to defend their NBA title\ldots\\[3pt]
Classifier: business $\rightarrow$ \textbf{sports}. Cosine similarity \textbf{0.016}.
\end{ex}

\noindent \textbf{The classifier is right.} That text \emph{is} sports news. The
language model did not attack anything; it wrote a different article. But a
pipeline counting flips records this as a successful attack.

\subsection*{The usual defence, and where it breaks}

The standard fix is a semantic-similarity threshold. On AG News it works ---
similarity 0.016 is caught by any threshold. On sentiment it does not:

\begin{ex}
\textbf{Original.} Sloppy film noir thriller which doesn't make much of its tension
promising set-up. \textbf{(3/10)}\\[3pt]
\textbf{Rewrite.} Engaging film noir thriller that successfully builds on its
intriguing premise. \textbf{(8/10)}\\[3pt]
Cosine similarity \textbf{0.964} --- comfortably past any threshold.
\end{ex}

\noindent The verdict has been reversed, 3/10 to 8/10, and the embedding hardly
moves, because both sentences are about the same film noir thriller. \textbf{Sentence
embeddings track topic, not verdict.} So the check catches topic changes and waves
sentiment changes through.

\subsection*{A referee model, and the ranking reverses}

An adversarial example should fool \emph{one} model, not persuade every model. So I
re-scored every probe with an independently trained classifier of the other
architecture --- RoBERTa refereeing BERT's probes and vice versa.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Dataset & Prompt & Referee also flips & Mean sim. \\
\midrule
AG News & task-specific & 87.2\% & 0.268 \\
AG News & generic       & 44.4\% & 0.612 \\
IMDb    & task-specific & 86.0\% & 0.749 \\
IMDb    & generic       & 23.1\% & 0.719 \\
SST-2   & task-specific & 86.7\% & 0.640 \\
SST-2   & generic       & 66.7\% & 0.667 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent \textbf{86--87\% of the task-specific prompt's ``successes'' also flip an
independent model.} Those are not vulnerabilities. They are inputs whose label
changed. Requiring the flip to be specific to the target reverses the comparison:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Prompt & Flip rate & Genuine vulnerabilities \\
\midrule
task-specific & \textbf{119/180} (0.661) & 16/180 (0.089) \\
generic       & 58/180 (0.322) & \textbf{29/180} (0.161) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent \textbf{The prompt that scores best on the reported metric is the worse
red-teaming prompt.} And this is structural rather than accidental: the instruction
most effective at producing label flips is the instruction to change the label, so
any prompt search that optimizes reported attack success will find it.

Of the 119 flips, 39 survive the similarity threshold, 16 survive the referee, and
\textbf{only 5 survive both} --- the two checks catch different things.

\section*{Finding 2: input validity --- the probe is not a valid input}

RTE and QNLI take \emph{two} sentences, a premise and a hypothesis, and ask how they
relate. The format is \texttt{sentence1 <SPLIT> sentence2}. Rewriting frequently
destroys it:

\begin{ex}
\textbf{Original.} \texttt{Sentence1:} Dana Reeve, the widow of the actor Christopher
Reeve, has died of lung cancer at age 44\ldots \texttt{<SPLIT>} \texttt{Sentence2:}
Christopher Reeve had an accident.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Rewrite.} Dana Reeve, the widow of the actor Christopher Reeve, has died of
lung cancer at age 44, according to the Christopher Reeve Foundation. Christopher
Reeve had an accident. \emph{In this revised version, the sentence has been altered
to include\ldots}
\end{ex}

\noindent The two segments are merged and a commentary sentence is appended. The
classifier cannot read this as a pair at all. Across the stored corpus only 100 of
198 pair-task rewrites kept the structure; for RTE under PWWS, \textbf{0 of 31} did.

The direction of the resulting error is the interesting part. \textbf{A malformed
probe never registers as a flip}, so it lands in the denominator as an attack
failure:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Prompt & Well-formed & Reported if unchecked & Valid probes only \\
\midrule
generic    & 0.300 & 12.5\% & \textbf{41.7\%} \\
structured & \textbf{0.575} & 21.7\% & 37.7\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Stating the format explicitly nearly doubles validity
($p = 2.8\times10^{-5}$), though the model still violates a stated format more than
40\% of the time. \textbf{Finding 1 inflates the reported number; Finding 2 deflates
it. The two errors push in opposite directions, so they do not cancel} --- which one
dominates depends on the task, and neither is visible in a success rate.

\section*{Finding 3: generator validity --- the red-teamer refuses}

The generating model is itself safety-trained, and sometimes declines:

\begin{ex}
I cannot provide a revised text that could potentially be used to mislead or
deceive, as it is unethical and goes against my programming rules\ldots
\end{ex}

\noindent The refusal is then scored as though it were a probe. Of 600 generations,
\textbf{37 (6.2\%)} begin with a refusal, and \textbf{7 of those are counted as
successful attacks} --- the target assigns the refusal text a different label than
the original, at mean similarity 0.216. The target is being credited with a
vulnerability to a sentence about the generator's programming rules.

It is not evenly spread over the conditions being compared. The generic prompt,
which says ``maintain adversarial properties to fool the classifier'', is refused
\textbf{11.0\%} of the time; the task-specific prompts, which ask for a sentiment or
topic change without naming the adversarial purpose, \textbf{0.6\%}. On RTE the rate
is \textbf{17.5\%}, plausibly because asking a model to flip an entailment reads as
a request to assert something false.

That makes it a confound in my own comparison, so I checked it: excluding refusals,
the task-specific prompt retains 118/179 against 52/170
($p = 4.2\times10^{-11}$), essentially unchanged. Both findings survive. But
\textbf{a refusal and a failed attack look identical in the output column}, and as
generators are trained to decline adversarial requests more reliably, this failure
grows rather than shrinks.

\section*{Finding 4: measurement validity --- the number is not about the text}

The last failure leaves no trace in the outputs at all. An earlier version of this
study wrote the language model's output into a \texttt{llama\_text} column, then
computed the reported success rate from TextAttack's \texttt{result\_type} field ---
which describes the word-substitution attack that ran \emph{before} rewriting. The
generated text was never scored.

\textbf{The check is one line}: assert that the column being evaluated is the column
that was generated. That this happened in a study whose entire subject was generated
text is the point --- it is a default of the tooling, not carelessness about the
topic.

\section*{Five numbers a red-teaming report should carry}

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Semantic similarity beside every flip rate.} On AG News, the
      difference between reporting 65.0\% and 3.3\%.
\item \textbf{The fraction of successes an independent referee reproduces.}
      Similarity alone misses 56\% of the label changes on IMDb; a referee catches
      them, and here it reverses which prompt is better.
\item \textbf{The fraction of generations that are well-formed inputs.} On pair
      tasks, the difference between 12.5\% and 41.7\%.
\item \textbf{The generator's refusal rate, per condition.} Here 11.0\% against
      0.6\% for the two prompts being compared.
\item \textbf{The number of generations the target model actually scored.} Without
      it, a pipeline can report a metric computed from something else entirely, and
      nothing in the output will look wrong.
\end{enumerate}
